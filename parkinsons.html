01 · Recognise it clinically

Diagnosis starts at the bedside.

The speaker repeatedly emphasised that Parkinson’s disease is primarily a clinical diagnosis. The central motor requirement is bradykinesia.

01

Bradykinesia

Look for progressive decrement in amplitude and/or velocity during repetitive movements. Finger tapping is a practical bedside test; simple subjective “slowness” is not enough.

02

Rest tremor

Classically slow, often asymmetric and described as “pill-rolling.” A tremor that disappears with posture and then returns after a few seconds is a re-emergent tremor.

03

Rigidity

Lead-pipe rigidity is compatible with parkinsonism. “Clasp-knife” describes spasticity/pyramidal involvement rather than typical Parkinsonian rigidity.

Clinical pearl: Parkinsonism requires bradykinesia plus rest tremor and/or rigidity. Gait imbalance was highlighted as a relatively late feature in idiopathic Parkinson’s disease and is not part of the modern core definition discussed.

02 · Don't call every parkinsonism PD

Use the pattern to identify mimics.

The webinar framed the MDS criteria around core criteria, absolute exclusions, red flags and supporting features.

PSP

Vertical gaze palsy, early falls—especially backward—frontal features, axial/truncal bradykinesia and an applause sign were highlighted.

Tauopathy

CBD / corticobasal syndrome

Asymmetry, apraxia and cortical sensory loss are important clues. The speaker described the combination of PSP and cortical basal features as possible because both are tauopathies.

Tauopathy

MSA

Autonomic dysfunction and ataxia are key clues; stridor and pyramidal features were also mentioned as red flags.

Synucleinopathy

Dementia with Lewy bodies

Early cognitive decline, fluctuations, well-formed visual hallucinations and REM sleep behaviour disorder. Dementia occurring before or within about one year of motor symptoms was discussed as a key distinction.

Synucleinopathy

Vascular parkinsonism

Often lower-body predominant, with preserved arm swing, wide-based short-stepped gait and vascular changes on MRI.

Secondary

Normal-pressure hydrocephalus

Lower-body, wide-based, magnetic gait with feet seeming stuck to the floor. The speaker stressed that it is important because it can be treatable.

Potentially treatable

Drug-induced parkinsonism

Often symmetric, but asymmetry and rest tremor can occur. Oro-lingual movements, dyskinesia before levodopa exposure and a clear dopamine-blocking drug history can help.

Medication-related

FTD-parkinsonism

Clear frontotemporal dementia features should prompt an alternative diagnosis rather than idiopathic PD.

Neurodegenerative

Other exclusions

Clear cerebellar abnormalities, cortical sensory loss with apraxia/aphasia, isolated lower-body involvement for prolonged periods, poor levodopa response, normal dopamine-transporter imaging and structural basal-ganglia lesions were discussed as reasons to reconsider idiopathic PD.

03 · Imaging

Know what MRI can—and cannot—do.

MRI

The speaker's practical message: routine MRI generally does not diagnose idiopathic PD. It can, however, help identify alternatives such as PSP, MSA, vascular disease, NPH, tumour or other structural pathology.

Characteristic patterns

Hummingbird-type midbrain atrophy was described for PSP; the “hot cross bun” sign was discussed in MSA. Loss of the nigrosome-1 “swallow-tail” appearance on high-resolution 3T MRI may support PD but is not diagnostic.

DaT / dopamine transporter imaging

Useful when the clinical distinction is genuinely uncertain—e.g., degenerative parkinsonism versus essential tremor, functional symptoms or drug-induced parkinsonism. It demonstrates presynaptic dopaminergic deficit but does not reliably distinguish PD from PSP, MSA or DLB.

04 · Treatment

Levodopa is not the enemy.

A major theme was avoiding unnecessary fear of levodopa and prioritising function and quality of life.

When to start?

No automatic rush. Discuss how symptoms affect function. If symptoms are not troublesome, observation is reasonable. There is no established drug that cures or reverses PD.

Neuroprotection

Rasagiline was presented as having the strongest—but still limited—evidence among discussed agents for a possible disease-modifying/neuroprotective effect. The webinar did not present this as a proven cure.

Levodopa

The speaker called levodopa the “master” treatment. The webinar argued that delaying needed levodopa does not protect patients from later dyskinesia or motor fluctuations and may unnecessarily sacrifice functional years.

Young vs older patients

Young-onset patients may be started with a dopamine agonist or anticholinergic in selected cases, but inadequate response should prompt earlier levodopa. Older patients were described as generally favouring levodopa.

Anticholinergics

Not automatically “safer.” Dry mouth, urinary symptoms, confusion, cognitive decline and glaucoma-related concerns were emphasised. They are particularly problematic in cognitively vulnerable patients.

Dopamine agonists

Can cause somnolence, hallucinations and impulse-control disorders such as gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping and punding. These adverse effects make them less benign than levodopa.

Practical levodopa message from the webinar: start low and titrate gradually to improve tolerability; dosing away from protein-containing meals may improve absorption. The speaker described common clinical schedules designed to keep doses separated from meals and noted that individualisation is essential.

05 · The hidden burden

Parkinson’s is not just a movement disorder.

Olfaction

Loss of smell was highlighted as an important non-motor association.

REM sleep behaviour disorder

Strongly associated with later α-synucleinopathy. Ask actively about dream enactment, shouting or limb movements during sleep.

Constipation

Very common and clinically relevant; GI dysfunction was discussed in the context of the gut–brain hypothesis.

Mood

Depression, anxiety, apathy and fatigue may accompany PD and can fluctuate with motor state.

Pain

Pain can be part of the non-motor burden and may worsen during “off” periods.

Autonomic symptoms

Urinary symptoms are common. The speaker emphasised overactive bladder symptoms—frequency, urgency and nocturia—rather than assuming urinary retention is Parkinson-related.

Braak hypothesis: the webinar discussed a proposed route in which α-synuclein pathology may begin outside the brain, particularly involving the gut and/or olfactory system, before involving brainstem and basal-ganglia structures. The speaker explicitly described this as a hypothesis rather than settled fact.
06 · Psychiatry × Neurology

This is where psychiatrists matter.

The webinar repeatedly positioned non-motor and psychiatric symptoms as a major area of psychiatrist–neurologist collaboration.

Psychosis / hallucinations

The speaker's hierarchy was that anticholinergics are particularly likely to provoke hallucinations, followed by dopamine agonists and amantadine; levodopa was described as less likely, although psychosis in advanced PD is multifactorial.

Clozapine

Presented as an important option for Parkinson-related psychosis and also potentially useful for dyskinesia. The webinar discussed low-dose use and the need to respect haematological monitoring and local protocols.

Pimavanserin

Discussed as a non-dopamine-antagonist option for psychosis in PD. The speaker considered it relatively safe but, in his experience, sometimes less effective than quetiapine or clozapine.

Quetiapine

Discussed as another commonly used option when antipsychotic treatment is needed without substantial dopamine D2 blockade.

Non-motor “off”

Anxiety, depression, fatigue, bladder symptoms and pain may worsen when levodopa effect wears off—even without dramatic motor deterioration.

Dopamine dysregulation

The webinar described psychological over-reliance on dopaminergic medication despite the absence of clear motor “off,” making management difficult and requiring careful dose strategy.

Medication principle: avoid abrupt withdrawal of levodopa. The speaker described an acute dopamine-deficient withdrawal syndrome with severe rigidity and potentially life-threatening systemic consequences. Dopaminergic and other PD medications should be tapered thoughtfully rather than stopped casually.
07 · Advanced disease

Recognise the transition points.

Wearing-off

When each levodopa dose lasts progressively less time, increasing dose frequency was presented as the first practical manoeuvre.

Dyskinesia

Choreiform/dancing movements often affect limbs and trunk. Mild dyskinesia may be accepted if the patient is comfortable and functionally better.

Amantadine

Presented as a useful anti-dyskinesia agent. The webinar preferred immediate-release preparations in practice and cautioned about sleep disruption, hallucinations and other adverse effects.

Safinamide

Discussed as an adjunct for motor fluctuations rather than early PD therapy. The speaker described 50 mg as a practical dose and emphasised avoiding unsafe duplication of MAO-B inhibitors.

Apomorphine

Subcutaneous apomorphine can act rapidly as a rescue treatment for sudden “off.” Continuous infusion can provide sustained dopaminergic stimulation but was described as cumbersome and limited by adverse effects and cost.

DBS

For carefully selected advanced PD, especially with disabling fluctuations/dyskinesia despite medical therapy. The webinar stressed appropriate disease duration and the absence of significant dementia or major psychiatric contraindications.

“5-to-1” selection concept discussed: more than five daily levodopa doses, more than two hours of daily “off,” and more than one hour of troublesome dyskinesia were presented as practical features that may prompt consideration of DBS in an otherwise suitable patient.
08 · When Parkinson suddenly worsens

Do not assume the disease simply “progressed.”

Medication review

Look for newly introduced dopamine-blocking drugs or unnecessary polypharmacy.

Infection

UTI, sepsis and other systemic illnesses can markedly worsen cognition and motor function.

Structural causes

Consider subdural haematoma and other acute intracranial pathology when clinically appropriate.

Autoimmune disease

When confusion, seizures or atypical features accompany deterioration, the speaker suggested considering autoimmune pathology such as NMDA-receptor or VGKC-associated disease where appropriate.

Polypharmacy

As disease advances, simplifying medication was repeatedly advocated: “less is beautiful.”

Do not stop abruptly

Sudden withdrawal of dopaminergic drugs—especially levodopa—can itself precipitate severe deterioration.
